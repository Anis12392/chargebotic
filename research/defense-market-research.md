# Defense Market Research — Chargebotic Tethered Powerline Drone

**Date:** June 2026  
**Product:** Tethered drone system that attaches to overhead power lines, harvests energy, and delivers it via tether to a ground battery station  
**Market focus:** US Defense (primary)

---

## 1. THE PROBLEM — WHY THIS MATTERS TO THE MILITARY

The US military has a critical and well-documented energy logistics problem:

- **$400/gallon** — the fully-burdened cost of fuel delivered to a remote forward operating base (FOB), vs. ~$3/gallon at the pump
- **$20B+/year** — total DoD annual spend on fuel and electricity
- **$20.2B/year** — just on air conditioning structures in Iraq and Afghanistan
- **70–80%** of resupply convoy weight is fuel and water — crowding out ammunition and food
- **1 casualty per 24 fuel resupply convoys** in Afghanistan — fuel logistics directly kills soldiers
- **50%+ of convoy casualties** in Afghanistan in 2009 were tied to fuel resupply missions

Diesel generators dominate FOB power today. They are loud, fuel-hungry, require constant maintenance, and create a constant logistics tail. The Army, SOCOM, and Marines all have active programs to reduce fuel dependency — Chargebotic directly answers this need.

**Where power lines exist near military zones:** US bases, training facilities (CONUS), bases in allied territories (Europe, Korea, Japan), and forward staging areas near infrastructure. The US has **750+ overseas bases** in 80+ countries, many near existing power infrastructure.

## 3. THE CUSTOMERS — WHO THEY ARE, HOW MANY, WHAT THEY DO

### Customer 1: US Army (Primary)

**Who they are:** The world's largest conventional land force, running the most FOBs and consuming the most fuel in theater.

**Numbers:**
- 32 active-duty Brigade Combat Teams (BCTs) + 27 National Guard BCTs = **59 BCTs total**
- Each BCT = 4,000–4,700 soldiers, commanded by a Colonel
- BCT types: 14 Infantry (IBCT), 11 Armored (ABCT), 7 Stryker (SBCT) — active duty

**What they do:** BCTs operate FOBs and patrol bases that require continuous power for comms, surveillance, command operations, and increasingly autonomous systems. They are the largest single buyer of power generation equipment in the US military.

**Pain point for Chargebotic:** BCTs near power infrastructure (training in CONUS, deployed near allied grid) are still running diesel generators because tapping power lines is not currently possible without Chargebotic's solution.

**How they buy:** 
- Program Executive Office Combat Support & Combat Service Support (PEO CS&CSS)
- Army SBIR/STTR Program ($250K Phase I → $1.7M Phase II)
- Other Transaction Authority (OTA) — faster procurement for innovative tech
- Direct contract via Army Futures Command (AFC)

---

### Customer 2: USSOCOM — Special Operations Command (High Priority)

**Who they are:** The elite joint command coordinating Army Special Forces (Green Berets), Navy SEALs, Rangers, MARSOC, and Air Force Special Operations. They operate in the most austere, denied environments with the least logistics support.

**Numbers:**
- ~73,000 SOF personnel total (active + reserve + civilian)
- Operates across 80+ countries simultaneously
- Has its own acquisition authority under Title 10, Section 167

**What they do:** SOCOM runs covert missions (special reconnaissance, counterterrorism, direct action) in locations where fuel resupply convoys are impossible or too dangerous. They have the most acute version of the energy problem — a small team operating for days or weeks with zero resupply. They pioneered energy harvesting research (TALOS program, EHAP assault pack).

**Pain point for Chargebotic:** Small teams near power line infrastructure (roads, villages, bases) could plug into existing grid energy with a single compact drone system, enabling extended operations without resupply.

**How they buy:**
- SOCOM SBIR: Phase I $150K (6 months), Phase II up to $1M (2 years)
- SOF AT&L (Acquisition, Technology and Logistics) — streamlined acquisitions
- Direct engagement with SOCOM Science & Technology (S&T) directorate
- Much faster than conventional Army procurement

---

### Customer 3: US Marine Corps (Secondary)

**Who they are:** Expeditionary force-in-readiness, optimized for rapid deployment from sea. Marine Expeditionary Units (MEUs) operate with minimal logistics tail.

**Numbers:**
- 7 active Marine Expeditionary Units (MEUs)
- ~177,000 active Marines total

**What they do:** MEUs go ashore quickly and need to establish power at austere sites rapidly. Marines have been especially vocal about reducing fuel dependency (USMC FOB Energy program). They build expeditionary FOBs that need immediate power before supply chains are established.

**Pain point for Chargebotic:** Rapid power establishment near coastal/urban infrastructure (which always has power lines) without waiting for fuel logistics.

**How they buy:**
- Marine Corps Systems Command (MARCORSYSCOM)
- SBIR/OTA pathways similar to Army
- Often co-buy with Army on shared programs

---

### Customer 4: Allied Forces / NATO Partners (International)

**Who they are:** NATO member militaries operating alongside US forces or on similar doctrine.

**Numbers:**
- 32 NATO member countries
- US allies in Pacific: Japan, South Korea, Australia — all running similar FOB structures

**What they do:** Face the same fuel logistics problem. Often buy US-developed defense tech through Foreign Military Sales (FMS) or direct commercial sale.

**How they buy:** Foreign Military Sales (FMS) through the US government, or direct commercial export with proper licensing.

## 5. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

### Direct Competitors (Powerline Energy Harvesting Drone)

**None identified.** No commercial or defense product currently offers autonomous drone-based power line tapping that delivers energy to a ground station via tether. This is a white space.

Academic research exists (University of Southern Denmark demonstrated autonomous drone charging from power lines in 2024), but it targets drone self-charging — not delivering power to a ground station. US Patent 7,563,124 covers low-voltage power line harvesting conceptually.

---

### Adjacent Competitors (Alternative Field Power)

| Company | What they do | Funding | Difference from Chargebotic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Windlift** | Tethered drone that generates power from wind, delivers via tether to ground | $24M+ DoD, $30M contract | Uses wind (weather-dependent, not always available); doesn't use existing infrastructure |
| **DARPA POWER** (RTX/Raytheon, Draper, BEAM) | Laser-based wireless power relay using drones as relay stations | $10M DARPA contract | Wireless/optical, totally different physics, targets long-range power beaming not ground station charging |
| **Solar + battery hybrids** | Deployed solar panels feeding battery banks | Widely adopted | Weather/light dependent; 30-50% fuel reduction, not elimination |
| **Diesel generators (MEP series)** | Standard Army generator sets | Incumbent | Fuel-dependent, loud, logistically expensive |

**Chargebotic's advantage over all of these:**  
Power lines are always-on, weather-independent, and already deployed near every human settlement and military base. Chargebotic is the only system that taps existing electrical infrastructure — no fuel, no weather dependency, no complex wireless physics.
